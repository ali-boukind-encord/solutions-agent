Show Fyxer how Encord replaces their spreadsheet-based email annotation process with a structured, scalable labeling pipeline - rendering emails natively, enforcing classification hierarchies, enabling entity extraction, and supporting QA - all built around the two workflows they care about most: email sorting and draft response flagging.

2. DEMO FLOW

STEP 1 - SET THE STAGE (2 minutes)
Before touching the screen, anchor the demo in their world. Say something like:
"What I want to show you today mirrors what your annotators are doing right now - taking an email, deciding how to classify it, flagging whether it needs a response, and pulling out key entities. The difference is that instead of a Google Sheet, everything is structured, enforced, and auditable."
This frames the demo as a workflow replacement, not a product tour.

STEP 2 - SHOW THE DATASET AND FILE RENDERING (5 minutes)
Navigate to: Encord platform > Datasets > "Fyxer - Email Dataset"
Show that the two sample emails are loaded: the Acme Corp contract renewal (deal/revenue) and the Productivity Weekly newsletter (marketing).
Click into the first file and show how the email renders. This is important - Albert specifically called out wanting to see how Encord handles emails in different formats. Walk through the rendering modes:
- Show the text rendering of the .txt file
- Explain that Encord supports HTML and PDF rendering as well, and can ingest JSON-structured data
- Say: "You mentioned JSON specifically - we can work with structured email exports, not just raw text. The rendering adapts to the format."
Highlight the metadata panel on the side. Even if metadata fields are empty for these demo files, point out where you would surface things like sender, subject line, timestamp - things they are currently tracking in columns in a spreadsheet.

STEP 3 - WALK THROUGH THE ONTOLOGY (8 minutes)
Navigate to: Ontologies > "Fyxer - Email Classification Ontology"
This is the heart of the demo. Go slowly here. Albert will appreciate the structure.

Start with the top-level classification:
"Email Category is the primary label - Important, Marketing, or Spam. This directly maps to your core sorting use case."

Then reveal the nesting. Click into "Important":
"When an annotator selects Important, two sub-questions automatically appear - Important Subcategory (Deal/Revenue, Customer Support, Partnership, Legal/Compliance) and Priority Level (High, Medium, Low). They cannot skip these - they are required. This is where your current process breaks down in sheets - there is nothing enforcing that an annotator fills in the right detail."
Pause and let that land. This directly addresses their accuracy and consistency pain.

Click into "Marketing" and show Campaign Type and Engagement Potential.
Then click into "Spam" and show Spam Confidence with the three-tier scale.
Say: "The reason we modeled Spam Confidence this way is that your annotators are the ones carrying the cognitive load of ambiguous cases. Rather than letting that ambiguity disappear into a binary label, you capture it. That feeds directly into your QA prioritization - low-confidence labels get reviewed first."

Move to "Requires Draft Response":
"This is your second major use case - flagging emails that need a drafted reply. If the annotator selects Yes, they are required to specify the tone of response and urgency level. If No, it closes out. This is the kind of structured signal you need to fine-tune a drafting model on user tone - you need labeled examples that go beyond just 'yes draft this.'"

Show "Annotator Confidence" as a standalone classification:
"This is a quality signal for your QA workflow. Right now Matt is manually reviewing everything. With this field, annotators self-report uncertainty, and you can build a review queue that surfaces only the Low - Needs Review cases automatically."

Show "Additional Notes" as the free-text field:
"This is the text-type attribute. Annotators can add unstructured context without it polluting your structured labels. It is optional but available."

STEP 4 - SHOW THE TEXT OBJECT AND ENTITY EXTRACTION (6 minutes)
Navigate to: the labeling editor with the Acme Corp email loaded
This is where you show the "Key Entity" text object. Highlight a span of text in the email - for example, highlight "March 28th" and assign it as Date/Deadline. Then highlight "$48,000 ACV" and assign it as Monetary Value. Then highlight "Sarah Johnson" and assign it as Person Name.
Go back and highlight an Action Item - for example "send over the updated contract with the revised pricing by end of day Friday" - and show the nested "Action Owner" attribute appear, where the annotator selects Recipient.
Say: "This is span-level annotation directly on the email body. Your annotators are not copying and pasting into a sheet - they are working in the document itself. And every entity they tag is structured, exportable, and tied to the specific text it came from."
This will resonate strongly because structured data extraction from emails is something Albert explicitly flagged.

3. KEY TALKING POINTS

Pain: Manual Google Sheets process, annotators copy-pasting
Talking point: In Encord, the email is the working document. Annotators label directly on the content. Nothing is copied anywhere. The structured output is generated automatically from the labels they apply.

Pain: Accuracy issues - wrong classification loses customers (a deal labeled as spam is lost revenue)
Talking point: The ontology enforces correct behavior. You cannot submit an Important email without specifying subcategory and priority. You cannot leave required fields blank. The structure prevents the exact class of error they are worried about. Show the required field validation.

Pain: Cognitive load on annotators
Talking point: Conditional nesting means annotators only see what is relevant to their current label. They are not scanning 30 columns in a sheet and guessing which ones apply. The interface guides them through the decision tree.

Pain: Inconsistent validation and founder-led QA is not scalable
Talking point: Annotator Confidence field + review queue means QA is systematic, not random. Surface the ambiguous cases first, let high-confidence labels pass faster. Matt stops being a bottleneck.

Pain: 20 fine-tuning use cases with no structured system
Talking point: Each use case gets its own project and ontology. They are independent but managed in one place. You get structure per task without chaos at the portfolio level.

Pain: Scalability beyond 15 annotators
Talking point: Workforce management, performance tracking, and workflow routing are native to Encord. Albert's annotation team can be onboarded and managed through the platform, not through ad hoc coordination.

Pain: Structured data extraction from emails specifically
Talking point: The Key Entity text object with nested attributes gives them span-level extraction tied to the document. Combined with JSON ingestion support, this is a proper extraction pipeline, not a workaround.

4. EXPECTED QUESTIONS AND OBJECTIONS

Q: How do you handle JSON email data specifically?
A: Encord can ingest JSON-structured data and render the relevant content fields in the labeling interface. You point the platform at the fields that contain the email body and metadata, and annotators work on that rendered content. This is worth following up with a technical deep-dive if needed - do not overpromise on the exact JSON parsing UI in this demo, but confirm the capability is real and offer a follow-up.

Q: Can we build our own ontologies from scratch or are we locked into templates?
A: Fully configurable. What you saw today was built specifically for Fyxer's use cases. Every attribute, nesting level, and option is defined by you. You can iterate on ontologies as your fine-tuning use cases evolve.

Q: How do you handle inter-annotator agreement across 15+ annotators?
A: Encord has consensus labeling - you can require multiple annotators to label the same item and flag disagreements automatically. Combined with the Annotator Confidence signal, you get both objective disagreement detection and subjective uncertainty flagging.

Q: What does the export look like? Can we use it directly for fine-tuning?
A: Labels export in structured JSON with full provenance - which annotator, what timestamp, every attribute value, and the span coordinates for text entities. This is directly usable as training data. If they have a specific fine-tuning framework or format in mind, note it and follow up with a sample export.

Q: How does this compare to [competitor they are evaluating]?

Q: What does pricing look like for our volume?

5. TECHNICAL NOTES

Ontology structure summary:
The ontology is text-type throughout, which matches the requirement. There are four classifications and one object type.

Classifications:
- Email Category (radio, required) with three-level nesting: Important has subcategory and priority; Marketing has campaign type and engagement potential; Spam has confidence level
- Requires Draft Response (radio, required) with nesting under Yes: tone and urgency
- Annotator Confidence (radio, required) - flat, three options
- Additional Notes (text, optional) - this is your text-type attribute, make sure to point this out explicitly as it satisfies the text type requirement

Object:
- Key Entity (text shape) with Entity Type radio attribute, and Action Item option carries a nested Action Owner sub-attribute. This is your span-level text annotation. Make sure the labeling editor is in text mode and you are able to click and drag to highlight spans before the call.

File types in demo:
Both files are .txt format. This is intentional for simplicity but be ready to explain that HTML, PDF, and JSON-structured email content are also supported. If Albert asks to see HTML rendering specifically, be honest about what is preloaded and offer a follow-up.

Metadata:
Metadata fields are empty in the demo files. This is fine for the demo but call out that in production they would populate these with sender, recipient, subject, date, and any other fields from their email pipeline. This replaces the metadata columns they currently manage in spreadsheets.

Things to watch out for:
- Albert knows this space. Do not pad the demo or stall. If you do not know an answer, say so and follow up.
- He is evaluating multiple vendors. Competitive positioning matters but do not get dragged into a features war. Stay on workflow value.
- The advisor in the room runs the annotator workforce commercially. If he asks about workforce management features or annotator performance reporting, lean into it - that is directly relevant to his business.
- Do not oversell JSON support without knowing the exact current state of that feature. Flag it as supported, offer a technical follow-up if he wants to go deep.
- Make sure the labeling editor loads cleanly with the email text visible and selectable before the call starts. Test the span annotation flow on the Acme email specifically.
- Keep the demo under 30 minutes of screen time. Albert is business-minded and will respect a tight, focused demo over a comprehensive product tour.
